How to Teach Sudoku to Kids: Start with Pictures

By Kidnova · Updated November 2026

Sudoku looks like a number puzzle, but it is really a logic puzzle: there is no math involved at all. That is why young children can learn it with pictures long before they are ready for number grids. Here is a simple way to introduce sudoku to kids, step by step.

Start with pictures, not numbers

For a 4 to 6 year old, numbers add an extra step that can get in the way. With pictures, like animals or fruit, children only need to notice what is already in the row and what is missing. Cut and paste picture puzzles also let children move a piece before they commit, which makes trying again easy.

Teach the one rule

Every row and every column has each picture once, with no repeats. Show your child one row with one empty space and ask: which picture is missing here? Then do the same with a column. Many children get it after two or three tries.

Begin with a small grid

A small grid, such as 4 squares across and 4 down, is a good first step. There are fewer things to keep in mind, so children can finish a puzzle in a few minutes and feel successful. Bigger grids can wait until the rule feels easy.

Solve the first puzzles together

Think out loud: this row has a cat, a dog and a fish, so the empty one must be the bird. When children hear how you reason, they learn the strategy, not just the answer. Then let them take turns choosing the next square.

Let mistakes happen

A wrong piece is not a problem, it is part of the puzzle. Instead of pointing at the mistake, ask: does this row have two of the same picture? Children who find their own mistakes build focus and confidence. Keep the answer pages for the end, so they can check their own work.

What sudoku helps with

Sudoku practices logical thinking, attention to detail, patience and visual scanning. Cutting and pasting the pieces also gives little hands fine motor practice, which helps with holding a pencil later.

When to move to number sudoku

When your child solves picture puzzles easily and starts asking for harder ones, try a small grid with the numbers 1 to 4. The rule stays exactly the same, so the step feels natural.

Final thoughts

Keep sessions short, celebrate every finished puzzle and stop before your child gets tired. A child who enjoys one puzzle a day is learning to think step by step, and having fun doing it.
