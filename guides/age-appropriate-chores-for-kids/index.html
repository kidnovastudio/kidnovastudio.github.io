Age-Appropriate Chores for Kids: A Simple Guide from 2 to 10

By Kidnova · Updated November 2026

Chores are not only about a tidy house. When children help at home, they learn that they are capable and that the family counts on them. The key is choosing chores that match their age, so they can succeed. Here is a simple guide by age, plus tips that make chores a habit instead of a daily argument.

Ages 2 to 3: little helpers

Toddlers love to copy adults, so turn that into help. Good first chores: put toys in a basket, throw rubbish in the bin, put dirty clothes in the laundry basket, wipe up small spills, and help feed a pet with an adult. Expect to do it together at this age.

Ages 4 to 5: simple daily jobs

Preschoolers can do short tasks on their own: make their bed (it does not need to be perfect), set napkins and spoons on the table, water plants, match socks and put books back on the shelf. A picture chart helps, because many children this age cannot read yet.

Ages 6 to 7: a real routine

Children this age can follow a weekly list. Try: pack their school bag, set and clear the table, fold small laundry, tidy their room and feed pets. A weekly chart with 5 to 7 chores is enough.

Ages 8 to 10: bigger responsibilities

Older children can vacuum a room, help cook simple meals, take out the rubbish, sort their own laundry and plan their day. This is also a good age to plan homework and activities on paper.

What if my child refuses?

Resistance is normal, especially when a chore is new. Keep your tone calm and matter-of-fact, offer a small choice (do you want to feed the cat or water the plants first?), and work side by side for the first few days. A visual chart helps too: children argue less with a chart than with a parent, because the plan is already decided.

Tips that make chores stick

Keep the list short: 3 to 5 daily chores is plenty. Show how to do each job the first few times. Put the chart where your child can see it, and praise effort, not perfection. Do chores at the same time each day, for example right after breakfast, so they become part of the routine.

Should kids get paid for chores?

Families do this differently, and both ways can work. Many parents keep everyday family jobs unpaid, then offer money for extra chores. If you want to connect chores and money, a pay per chore chart makes it clear and fair.

Final thoughts

Start small, stay consistent and celebrate progress. A child who feeds the cat every morning at 5 is learning responsibility that will help them for years.
