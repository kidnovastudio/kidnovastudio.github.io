How to Teach Kids About Money: Allowance, Saving and Goals

By Kidnova · Updated November 2026

Children start forming ideas about money early, long before they have a bank account. You do not need to be a finance expert to teach them. A few simple habits at home, like saving for a goal or earning money for extra chores, give children a strong start. Here is how to do it step by step.

Ages 3 to 5: money is real

Let your child hold coins, sort them and count them. Use a clear jar so they can see their money grow. Simple ideas to share: we use money to buy things, and sometimes we wait and save for something special.

Ages 6 to 8: save for a goal

Help your child choose one goal, like a toy or a book, and write down how much it costs. A visual tracker they can color in every time they save makes the goal feel real and teaches patience.

Ages 9 and up: plan and choose

Older children can manage a small weekly budget, compare prices and decide between two things. Let them make small mistakes now, when the cost is low, and talk about what they would do differently next time.

Allowance or pay per chore?

There is no single right answer. A fixed allowance teaches budgeting, because children plan with the same amount each week. Paying for extra chores shows that money comes from work. Many families mix both: a small allowance plus paid extra jobs.

Try three jars: spend, save, give

Give your child three jars or envelopes. Each time they get money, they split it: some to spend now, some to save for a goal and some to give to a cause they care about. It turns three big ideas into one simple routine.

Common mistakes to avoid

Rescuing every time: if your child spends all their money and regrets it, that lesson is valuable, so try not to top it up. Goals that are too far away: young children lose interest if a goal takes months, so start with something they can reach in a few weeks. Keeping it secret: children learn by watching, so it helps to talk openly about simple choices like saving for a holiday.

Talk about money in everyday life

At the shop, think out loud: this one costs more, so I will buy the smaller one. Small, honest conversations teach more than one big lesson, and they show children that money decisions are normal.

Final thoughts

Keep it simple, visual and regular. When children see their savings grow and make their own small choices, they build money habits that last.
