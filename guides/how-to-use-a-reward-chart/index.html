How to Use a Reward Chart for Kids (So It Actually Works)

By Kidnova · Updated November 2026

A reward chart is one of the simplest tools a parent can use: a sheet of paper, a few stickers and a clear goal. Used well, it helps children see their progress and feel proud of it. Used badly, it turns into a daily argument about stickers. The difference is usually in the setup. Here is how to make a reward chart work for your child.

1. Pick one clear goal

Start with one behavior, not five. Say it in a positive way that your child can picture: "put your pajamas on by yourself" works better than "be good at bedtime". When the first goal becomes easy, you can add a second one.

2. Make the steps small

Young children need to win early. If your child never stays in bed, aim for one good night first, not a whole week. A chart with 5 to 10 spaces is a good size for most children, because the end is close enough to stay exciting.

3. Choose the reward together

Ask your child what they would like to work for and agree on it before you start. Small rewards work best: choosing the bedtime story, a trip to the park, baking together or an extra 10 minutes of play. Time together often means more to children than a new toy.

4. Give the sticker right away

The sticker works because it comes straight after the good behavior. Let your child put it on the chart themselves, and say exactly what they did: "You brushed your teeth without a reminder. That's a sticker!" Specific praise teaches more than "good job".

5. Never take stickers away

A sticker earned is a sticker kept. Taking stickers off the chart for bad behavior makes the chart feel like a punishment, and children often give up on it. If there is a bad day, simply start fresh the next morning.

6. Keep it where your child can see it

Put the chart at your child's eye level, on the fridge or a bedroom door. Looking at a chart that is filling up is part of the motivation, and it reminds both of you to use it.

7. Phase the chart out

A reward chart is a starter, not a forever plan. Once the new habit is easy, make the steps bigger (two good days for one sticker), then stop the chart and keep the praise. Many families then start a new chart for the next goal.

Reward chart or chore chart?

A reward chart builds one new habit or behavior with stickers and a prize at the end. A chore chart lists regular jobs for the week so children know what to do each day. Some families use both: a chore chart for the routine, and a reward chart for the one thing they are working on right now.

Final thoughts

Keep the goal small, the reward simple and the praise specific. A reward chart is not about bribing children, it is about helping them notice their own progress until the habit no longer needs a sticker.
